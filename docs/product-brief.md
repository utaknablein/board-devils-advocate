# Product brief

## The problem

Boards approve proposals that were never seriously challenged. Not because directors lack judgment, but because challenge is socially expensive. The sponsor is a colleague, the deck took months, the deadline is real, and the director who asks the awkward question pays for it in goodwill. Groupthink is rarely loud. It is a room full of capable people each deciding the question can wait.

Independent challenge already exists in pieces: internal audit looks backward, external advisers are often paid by the side that wants approval, and board evaluations happen once a year. Nothing sits between the proposal and the vote, every time.

## The product

Board Devil's Advocate is an independent AI challenger. Every proposal headed to the board is run through the same eight lenses, and directors receive a challenge brief with the pack. The challenge is routine, so asking the hard question stops being personal.

## Who buys it

- **Board chairs and lead independent directors** who want better deliberation without adding meetings.
- **Company secretaries and general counsel** who own the board process and want a defensible record that proposals were scrutinized.
- **Private equity and family office boards**, where deal cadence is high and sponsor pressure is strong.
- **Regulated boards** (banking, insurance, healthcare) where supervisors increasingly expect evidence of effective challenge.

## Trust model

The product only works if directors trust it more than management does. That shapes the design:

- The brief goes to the board, not through management. Management cannot edit it.
- The challenger's instructions are published and versioned.
- It never recommends a vote, so it cannot become a political instrument.
- Client data is isolated. Pattern memory is computed only from a board's own history and is never pooled across clients.
- Deployment options include private cloud and zero-retention model access for sensitive boards.

## Premium enterprise tier: pattern memory

The long-term value is institutional memory. Each board accumulates a private **decision ledger**: proposal, challenge, decision, conditions attached and, at review points, actual outcomes against plan. Once the ledger has enough history, the challenger adds a ninth section to every brief: **"What this board's own history says."**

Examples of the patterns it can surface:

- Approvals made under externally imposed deadlines, and how they performed.
- Programs where the sponsor also owned the benefits target.
- Proposals presented without alternatives, and how often they returned for more money.
- Pilots scaled without a second test, and how scaled results compared.

This turns the board's past into a live asset instead of something that leaves with each departing director.

## Roadmap

| Phase | Scope |
|-------|-------|
| **1. Challenge brief** | Single proposal in, eight-lens brief out. Board portal export. (This demo.) |
| **2. Board workflow** | Integration with board portals, company secretary intake, versioned briefs, audit trail of what was challenged and what conditions were attached. |
| **3. Decision ledger** | Structured capture of decisions and scheduled outcome reviews at 6, 12 and 24 months. |
| **4. Pattern memory** | Cross-decision analysis within each client's own ledger; "what this board's history says" section in every brief. |
| **5. Committee packs** | Tailored lens sets for audit, risk and remuneration committees, and sector-specific regulatory lenses. |

## Open questions to test with buyers

- Who is the economic buyer: the chair, the company secretary, or the general counsel?
- Does management see the brief before the meeting, at the same time as the board, or not at all?
- How much outcome data will boards commit to recording, and who owns the review cadence?
- Which regulated sectors will value a documented challenge record most, and what format do supervisors expect?
